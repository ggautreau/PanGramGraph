11 Escherichia coli · anchored on dnaA

Origin Fork

Eleven E. coli chromosomes, rotated so each begins at its replication initiator, then walked gene by gene. Families that every strain carries form the spine. Where the strains part, the graph forks. Dashed edges are the ones no strain has: adjacencies a genomic language model expected to find there. Given this anchor, the model names the next gene correctly more than half the time along the spine, and roughly one time in six once the graph opens. Its guesses are not noise: looked up in 2,002 genomes, they exist 2.7 times more often than chance.

Start at the left. Columns 1 to 5 run orange — dnaN, recF, gyrB, yidB, yidA — the chromosome is identical in all eleven strains and the model names the first three without error. Two details sit right there and show the colour code doing its work. Column 0, dnaA itself, is green rather than orange: only nine strains carry a DnaA protein, because RefSeq calls the gene a pseudogene in the other two. And under yidB hangs a lone blue box, an IS3 transposase that exactly one strain has inserted there. Around column 12 the boxes start stacking and the dashes multiply. That is where a region of plasticity begins, and where the model loses the thread.

Confidently wrong is the normal failure. Open yidE at column 17 in APEC O1: the model puts 99.9% of its probability on ybjL and 0.02 bits of doubt, while the gene actually there sits at rank 1,031. It does not hedge when it is lost, it commits. That is why the bars on top stay flat, and why accuracy separates the conserved chromosome from the plastic one while confidence does not.

One family can hold several gene names. Families here are clusters in embedding space, so close paralogues merge: the box labelled dgoR is called lldR in some strains — both GntR-family regulators. The panel names the gene each strain actually carries when it differs from the box.

The colours are PPanGGOLiN’s, the classes are not. Here they are plain frequencies over eleven strains. PPanGGOLiN fits a statistical mixture over thousands of genomes, and its partition does not transfer to a subset. The section further down compares the two on the same chromosome.

Reading the fork

The spine holds for four genes. dnaN, recF, gyrB, yidB are core in all eleven strains, which is the replication cassette every bacterial chromosome carries next to its origin. The model names the first three of them in every single strain — eleven out of eleven at each step, with almost no probability left over for anything else. Then the graph opens.

Its certainty does not survive the opening. Across the first twelve loci the per-step accuracy runs 100, 100, 100, 0, 91, 9, 0, 0, 64, 64, 9, 18 percent. The drop is not a decay, it is a cliff, and it lands exactly where the strains stop agreeing.

The first fork is the dgo operon for galactonate catabolism: most strains carry it, O157:H7 Sakai does not and goes straight to cbrA. Further right the lanes reach the waa locus, which builds the lipopolysaccharide core and is among the most variable stretches of the species. By then almost every node is shell or cloud.

Two strains have no DnaA protein. RefSeq annotates dnaA as a pseudogene in CFT073 and Nissle 1917, so those lanes begin at dnaN and are shifted one locus to keep them aligned.

Against the reference pangenome

PanGBank 11587 · 2,002 genomes

The graph above is built from eleven strains. PPanGGOLiN has already partitioned E. coli over two thousand, and labels every gene of the O157:H7 Sakai chromosome persistent, shell or cloud, and marks its regions of genomic plasticity. Joining that to my walk on genomic coordinate — all 79 loci matched — tests the prediction this project started from.

The accuracy prediction holds. The model names the next gene five times more often outside a region of plasticity than inside one, and its success falls monotonically from persistent to shell to cloud. What it has learned really is where the chromosome is constrained, and an independently built pangenome agrees about where that is.

The confidence prediction fails. Entropy is 0.48 bits outside an RGP and 0.49 inside — no difference at all. The model is exactly as sure of itself in the plastic regions, where it is almost always wrong, as in the conserved ones. Its certainty tracks nothing useful. For anyone hoping to read plasticity off a language model's own uncertainty, that is the finding: accuracy carries the signal, confidence does not.

Small numbers, one strain. Seventy-nine loci on a single chromosome, five of them cloud. The direction is consistent and the RGP contrast is large, but these are not tight estimates.

Two of the eleven are not in that pangenome. GTDB R232 moved K-12 MG1655 out of E. coli altogether, into its own species cluster, and ST131 EC958 is in no PanGBank pangenome. The reference for the most studied bacterium in biology does not currently sit in its own species' pangenome.

Are the model’s guesses real?

PanGBank 11587 · 2,002 genomes

The dashed boxes are genes the model expected and none of the eleven strains carries there. Eleven genomes are a small sample of a species, so the fair test is to look them up in a much larger one. They are there far more often than chance. Predicted adjacencies turn up in the 2,002-genome PanGBank graph 2.7 times as often as the same target placed next to another source, and the effect holds for families that none of the eleven strains carries at all.

How the lookup works. Both graphs are put in one vocabulary. Every PanGBank family’s representative protein is embedded with ESM-2 and given the Bacformer family of its nearest real exemplar, if the cosine similarity reaches 0.95 (56,134 of 59,165 families). An edge a → x counts as found when some family mapped to x sits next to some family mapped to a in the PanGBank graph. Tick Only those found in PanGBank above the graph: the dashed boxes with a solid violet outline are the ones found.

Read it against the control. The same lookup finds only 60% of the adjacencies the eleven strains actually carry, which are certainly real. That 60% is the ceiling of the method, and 13.9% is about a quarter of it. The model’s softmax probability sorts its guesses too: 16.8% of the predictions above the median probability are found, against 10.9% of those below it.

What is not tested. Only 678 of the 1,343 predicted edges can be looked up. The other 655 point to a family outside the 9,814-family exemplar bank, and are left out, not counted as wrong. The chance line keeps the target and swaps the source. Keeping the source and drawing a random target gives 12.9×, which flatters the model, because the targets it predicts are well-connected families.

Every prefix handed to the model is the real protein embeddings of a real chromosome, so this measures the model, not a substitution. The anchor is what makes the difference. Along the replication cassette its first guess is right more than half the time, out of fifty thousand families, and its distribution is nearly a point mass. Past the fork the same model falls to about one guess in six. It has not learned a generic next-gene rule; it has learned the stretches of the chromosome that are actually constrained, and it knows where they stop. The dashed edges are what the rest of the error looks like laid on the graph.

Families are assigned by nearest real protein in embedding space, not by the model's masked head. Across these strains the masked head puts orthologues in the same family in none of the cases tested, because it reads genomic context as well as sequence. Nearest-exemplar agrees across all eleven strains for 90% of genes, and puts dnaA, dnaN, recF and gyrB each in one family.
